Introduction

This week you start working with physical circuits. Read this before class: we will not have time to cover it from scratch, and class time is for doing it with your hands. Class meets in the shop. Bring your safety glasses, your BOJACK kit and your project parts.

1. Safety

  • Safety glasses on whenever anyone near you is soldering or cutting leads. Three reasons:
    • Overheated parts can burst. Hold the iron on a component too long, or wire an electrolytic capacitor the wrong way round, and it can pop and spray hot debris.
    • Clipped leads fly across the room when you trim them. Hold the end as you cut.
    • Solder and flux spit, especially when a joint is reheated.
  • The iron tip is 300–400°C (570–750°F). Always return it to its stand. Never leave it lying on the table.
  • Never catch a falling iron. Step back and let it drop.
  • Fumes come from the flux, not the metal. Place the fume extractor close to the joint, a hand-width away, so it pulls the smoke away before it reaches your face. Keep your head to one side of the smoke, not above it.
  • Lead: if you use leaded solder, do not eat or drink at the bench, and wash your hands afterwards.
  • Unplug before you change anything. Disconnect USB or battery before moving wires.

2. Voltage, Current and Resistance

  • Voltage (V, volts): the push. This week your circuits run at 5V from the kit's power supply module; so does the Arduino from Week 7.
  • Current (I, amps): how much flows. Small parts use milliamps (mA).
  • Resistance (R, ohms, Ω): what limits the flow.
  • Ohm's law: V = I × R. If you know two, you can work out the third.

Worked example: an LED and its resistor

An LED always needs a resistor in series, or it draws too much current and dies. Keep it to a few milliamps: from Week 7, each Arduino UNO R4 pin should supply no more than 8 mA, so the same resistor works there too.

Supply:            5V
LED drop (red):    ~2V
Left for resistor: 5V - 2V = 3V
Target current:    3 mA (0.003 A), bright enough for an indicator
R = V / I = 3 / 0.003 = 1000 ohms  ->  use a 1 kΩ resistor

A short circuit is a path from power to ground with almost no resistance. It can burn wires and damage the board. If anything gets hot or the board resets, unplug immediately.

3. Breadboards: Test Before You Solder

A breadboard lets you build a circuit without soldering, so you can test and change it. Always get a circuit working on the breadboard before you solder anything.

How it is built: under the plastic, each short row of holes sits on one metal spring clip, and each power rail sits on one long metal strip. A wire pushed into a hole is gripped by the clip, and everything on the same clip is connected. Peel the backing off an old breadboard and you can see them.

  • Terminal strips: in the middle, each short row of 5 holes is connected. Rows on either side of the centre gap are not connected to each other.
  • Power rails: the long lines along the edges, marked + (red) and − (blue). Each rail runs the length of the board. On some boards the rail is split halfway; check with a multimeter.
  • The centre gap is where chips and some modules straddle, so each pin gets its own row.
  • Powering the breadboard: the BOJACK kit's power supply module plugs into one end of the board, its pins dropping into both pairs of rails. Each side has a jumper for 3.3V or 5V: set both to 5V. Power it through its barrel jack (6.5–12V, from a battery or adapter), and use its on/off switch to power the circuit up and down. Check its polarity: the module's + must sit in the red rail.
  • From Week 7 the Arduino can power the breadboard instead, from its 5V and GND pins.

4. Wire

Type What it is Use it for
Solid core One thick copper conductor. Holds its shape. Breadboard jumpers, wires that never move. Breaks if flexed repeatedly.
Stranded Many thin copper strands twisted together. Flexible. Anything that moves, bends or vibrates: lids, hinges, wearables, cables to a handheld part. Tin the end before pushing it into a breadboard.

Gauge (AWG): the smaller the number, the thicker the wire. 22 AWG is the standard for breadboards and signals. Use 18–20 AWG for motors and anything drawing more current.

Stripping: set the stripper to the wire's gauge, strip about 5–7 mm, and check no strands are cut. For stranded wire, twist the strands tight before tinning.

5. The Multimeter

  • Black lead in COM, red lead in VΩ.
  • DC voltage (V⎓): measure across a part, with the circuit powered. Expect ~5V between the rails.
  • Continuity (the beep): checks that two points are connected. Use it on every solder joint and to check breadboard rails. Circuit unpowered.
  • Resistance (Ω): check a resistor's value. Circuit unpowered, always.

6. Soldering

Solder is a metal alloy that melts at a lower temperature than the parts it joins. A good joint is mechanical and electrical at the same time.

Bench station or portable iron. The shop's stations sit on the bench with a stand and a temperature dial. Portable irons such as the TS80 run from a USB-C power bank (it needs a Quick Charge 3.0 bank that gives 9V), set their temperature digitally between 100 and 400°C (212–752°F), and take swappable tips. Useful for working away from the shop.

The iron and its tips

Tip Shape When to use it
Chisel, 1.6–2.4 mm Flat, like a small screwdriver The default. Header pins, through-hole parts, most joints. The flat face transfers heat well.
Large chisel, 3 mm+ Wide and flat Thick wires, big pads, battery tabs, anything that soaks up heat.
Conical, fine Pointed Very small, tightly spaced pins. Transfers heat poorly, so joints take longer. Not for beginners' general use.

Rule of thumb: use the biggest tip that fits the joint. More contact means faster heating, which means less heat soaking into the part.

Temperature

Solder Melts at Iron set to
Leaded 63/37 183°C / 361°F 315–345°C / 600–650°F
Lead-free (SAC305) ~217°C / 423°F 345–370°C / 650–700°F
  • Go cooler for small, heat-sensitive parts (LEDs, sensors, thin wires) and fine pins. Too hot burns the flux before it works, oxidises the tip, and can lift pads off a board or melt insulation.
  • Go hotter for thick wires, big pads and ground planes, which pull heat away. Too cool and you hold the iron on longer, which heats the part more, and the joint comes out dull and lumpy (a cold joint).
  • Aim for the temperature that finishes a joint in 2–3 seconds.

Solder types, for this class

  • Leaded 63/37 (tin/lead): melts at one exact point, flows easily, forgiving. Best for learning. Needs the lead hygiene above.
  • Lead-free (SAC305: tin/silver/copper): needs a hotter iron, flows less easily, joints look duller even when good. Required for products sold in many markets.
  • Diameter: 0.6–0.8 mm (0.025–0.031") for electronics. Thicker solder floods small joints.
  • Always rosin-core (flux inside). Never use acid-core plumbing solder on electronics: it corrodes the circuit.

Flux

Metal surfaces oxidise, and solder will not stick to oxide. Flux cleans the oxide off as it heats and helps the solder flow into the joint. Rosin-core solder already contains flux. A flux pen adds extra when reworking a joint or soldering wires that will not take solder. No-clean flux can be left on.

Cleaning flux: rosin flux leaves a sticky brown residue. Clean it off with isopropyl alcohol (90% or stronger) and an old toothbrush or cotton bud, then let it dry before you power the circuit. Clean boards are easier to inspect, and some flux residues attract moisture and slowly corrode joints.

Heat-shrink

Heat-shrink tubing insulates and strengthens a wire splice or an exposed joint.

  • Pick a diameter that slides easily over the joint. Most tubing shrinks to half its size (2:1).
  • Cut 10–15 mm, enough to cover the joint plus a few millimetres of insulation on each side.
  • Slide it on before you solder. Once both ends are joined, you cannot get it on.
  • Shrink it with a heat gun, not the iron. The iron melts it.

Technique

  1. Tin the tip: melt a little solder on the clean tip so it shines. A shiny tip transfers heat; a dull one does not.
  2. Heat the joint, not the solder: touch the tip to both the pad and the lead at once.
  3. After 1–2 seconds, feed solder into the joint (opposite side from the iron), not onto the tip.
  4. Remove the solder, then the iron. Do not move the parts for a few seconds while it cools.
  5. A good joint is shiny and shaped like a small volcano, wetting both surfaces. A ball sitting on top, or a dull grainy surface, is a cold joint: reheat it with a little flux.
  6. Wire to wire: tin both ends first, slide heat-shrink on before joining, then join and shrink.

7. When to Solder

  • Breadboard while you are still testing and changing the circuit.
  • Solder when a connection has to survive: parts that move or vibrate, wires going inside an enclosure, modules that come without pins, and your final prototype.
  • Many modules (sensors, drivers) come as a small PCB with loose header pins. Soldering the headers on is often the first soldering job of a project, and then the module can plug into a breadboard.

8. Perfboard: From Breadboard to Soldered

A perfboard is a board of holes on a 2.54 mm grid, the same spacing as a breadboard and as most component pins. Unlike a breadboard, the holes are not connected to each other: you make every connection with solder. It is the simplest way to turn a breadboard circuit into one that survives being handled.

  • Get it working on the breadboard first. Never design a circuit on perfboard.
  • Plan the layout on paper before you remove anything from the breadboard: one square per hole, each part drawn where it will sit. Keep connected parts next to each other so the joints are short.
  • Parts go on the top, solder on the bottom. Push the legs through, bend them slightly outward so the part stays put, then solder.
  • Connections: bend a part's own leg across to the next joint, or use a short piece of bare (tinned) wire. Use insulated wire where a connection has to cross another.
  • Wires off the board (to power, to the Arduino, to a button in a lid) are stranded, colour-coded (red for 5V, black for GND), and long enough to reach without strain.
  • Check before power: trim extra legs, look for solder bridges between holes that should not touch, and test with continuity. Then connect power and test that it behaves exactly as it did on the breadboard.

9. Documenting a Circuit in Fritzing

Fritzing draws a circuit the way it looks on a breadboard, so a classmate can rebuild it from your drawing. It is how parts are documented in the class library.

Fritzing is a paid download (about $12 for individuals, from fritzing.org/download). Buy and install it before class.

  1. In the Breadboard view, place a breadboard, a power source and your parts, and draw each wire. Use the same wire colours as your real circuit.
  2. From Week 7, when the Arduino joins your circuits: download arduino-UNO-R4-wifi.fzpz from the ACCD-Fritzing-Parts repo and open it in Fritzing. It adds the UNO R4 WiFi to your parts bin.
  3. Check the Schematic view. Fritzing builds it from your breadboard drawing; tidy it so the lines do not cross where they do not need to.
  4. If Fritzing has no part for your component, use the closest generic part and label it with a note.
  5. Save the .fzz file, and export the Breadboard view as a .jpg (File > Export > as Image).
