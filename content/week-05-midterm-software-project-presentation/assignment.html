The Midterm

You have been building the control interface for your project since Week 2, and last week you put it in front of classmates and watched them use it. This is where you show what happened.

This is 10% of your course grade. It is also the last checkpoint before the course turns to hardware — from Week 6 onward you are soldering, and the interface you present here is the one you will eventually connect to your device.

Format

  • 10 minutes minimum per person, plus discussion. Take the time — a thorough presentation is worth more than a brisk one.
  • Live on your laptop. Show the interface working, do not describe it.
  • Head of Department Lan Yu may be attending.

What to cover

Walk us through the arc. Five parts, in this order:

1. The project

What studio project you chose, and what your hero experience is — the one interaction your device exists to do. Show the original object. Say what it did not resolve.

2. Before

The version of your interface as it stood when you tested it. This matters: without showing the before, nobody can judge whether the after is an improvement.

3. The test

How you ran it. Who your participants were, which tasks you gave them, where those tasks came from in your requirements list, and what priority each carried.

4. Results

What actually happened. Your functionality scores per task and the emotional reactions you logged per element. Show the tables.

Then three findings: what you observed, what it means, and what you decided to do about it.

5. After

The current version, with at least one change you have already made because of the testing. Point at the specific thing and say which finding drove it.

What makes a strong presentation

A finding that surprised you. Something you were confident about that a participant broke. These are worth more than a clean run, because they are the only evidence that the testing did any work.

Honesty about what did not go well. A session where everything went smoothly usually means the tasks were too easy, or you helped too much, or your participants were being kind. Say so if that is what happened — recognising it is a better result than pretending otherwise.

Specificity about the change. "I improved the layout" tells us nothing. "Three of four participants hunted for a submit button, so I added one" tells us everything.

A working demonstration. Click through it in front of us. Screenshots of an interface are a description of an interface.

How this is graded

Graded on the functionality and clarity of the interface, and on the quality of the testing that informed it — not on how polished the visual design is.

A rough interface with sharp testing and a clear change will score better than a beautiful one presented without evidence. The point of this checkpoint is whether you can find out something true about your own work and act on it.
