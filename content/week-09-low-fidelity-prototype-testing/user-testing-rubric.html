User Testing: Rubric & Protocol

You will test your prototype three times, with three different groups of people, at three levels of prototype fidelity. This page is the instrument you use every time.

The rule that makes all of this work: you score the same things the same way in every round. That is the only reason round 3 can be compared to round 1, and that comparison is what you present in Week 13.

The four lenses, and the two we use

Google's UX framework evaluates a product against four questions:

  • Usable: is the design clear and easy to operate?
  • Equitable: does it work for people of differing abilities and backgrounds?
  • Enjoyable: does it create a positive connection?
  • Useful: does it solve a real problem?

This is the standard frame in industry. You will encounter it at Google, and versions of it at Apple, Logitech, and most product companies.

For this course we score two of them: usable and enjoyable. Usefulness is what your studio critique already interrogates. Equity matters and you should notice it — log it when it appears — but scoring it properly needs recruitment we cannot manage in fourteen weeks.

Alpha and beta

Companies test internally before they test externally. Alpha testing happens in-house, with people who share your context and vocabulary. Beta testing goes outside, to people who do not.

Your three rounds walk that distance deliberately. Each round moves further from your own assumptions.

Round Week Who you test with What they bring
1 — Alpha 9 Your classmates They know the project and share your vocabulary
2 — Beta, near 10 GradID students from other terms They know the discipline but not your project
3 — Beta, far 12 ArtCenter students outside product and industrial design (not GradID, not undergrad Product) They know neither. This is the honest test

4–5 participants in rounds 1 and 2, and 6–8 in round 3. Round 1 is capped by the size of the class (five students). Round 3 needs more people because it is the furthest from your assumptions and it is the result you present. Fewer than that and one unusual person skews everything.

One participant in round 1 must be naive — someone who has no idea what your device is. Do not explain it to them. Hand it over and watch. If they cannot work out what it is for, that is the most important thing you will learn all term, and you want to learn it in Week 9, not Week 12.

Prototype fidelity rises with each round

The prototype you test is not the same object each time. It develops alongside the testing, and each level lets you test things the previous one could not.

Round Prototype What it can validly test
1 Rough. Glue gun, Sharpie icons, wires exposed. Smoke and mirrors where needed. Does the concept read? Is the flow right? Where are the large blind spots?
2 Form. 3D printed in the intended geometry, buttons integrated, electronics behind. Visibly a print — no CMF, loose tolerances, some tape. Do the interactions work on the real form? Reach, position, sequence, grip. Which functionality is still struggling?
3 Realistic. Looks and feels close to a real product. CMF applied, intended colours, decaled icons. Everything above, plus desirability and tactile response. The hard problems that survived rounds 1 and 2.

You should be refining the 3D file continuously, using what the rough prototype teaches you to adjust it before you print for round 2.

Where your tasks come from

You do not invent test tasks. They come out of your HMI document.

Every requirement in that document is written as a user statement:

As a user, when I press and hold the dial, I expect the device to enter setup mode and show the current target, so that I can change the number of turns before starting.

That statement is a test task. Hand the participant the device and ask them to change the target. Do not tell them how.

Pick 3–5 tasks per round, covering every Must-have in your hero experience. Use the same tasks in every round — changing them destroys comparability.

Priority changes how you read a failure

Your HMI and software requirements should be prioritised:

  • Must have — the product is broken without it
  • Should have — expected, but the product still works without it
  • Nice to have — improves the experience, not essential
  • Dropped — considered and deliberately excluded

A Must-have that fails is a different order of problem from a Nice-to-have that fails. Record priority against every task so your severity ratings mean something.

Scoring, part 1: Functionality

Scored per step, with one row per participant and the steps across. Week 4 used one row per participant per step; from Week 9 the table turns, so each participant reads as one line.

One change from Week 4: a task's score is now the mean of its steps, not its worst step. That way smaller improvements show between rounds. The worst step still shows in the Severity column.

Task outcome

Score Outcome What it looks like
4 Completed unaided Straight through, no pause, no wrong turns
3 Completed with hesitation Got there, but paused, backtracked, or tried something wrong first
2 Completed with help Needed a hint from you to proceed
1 Abandoned or failed Gave up, or believed they had succeeded when they had not

Note that last one. A participant who thinks they completed the task but did not is a failure, and usually a more serious one than giving up — the device told them something untrue.

Time on task

Before testing, do an expert dry run: run every step yourself on the prototype and time it (record the run and replay it if that is easier). That time goes in the dry run row. For each participant, the time ratio is their time over yours. You know the device, so participants are always slower: the sheet flags a time amber at 3x your dry run and red at 5x. These are starting values in two input cells, and you can change them. What matters is the ratio, not the seconds: a step that takes you 5 seconds and a participant 40 is a finding.

Issue severity

For every issue observed, rate it. Severity stays your judgement; the sheet only hints. A Sev cell turns yellow when a score of 1 (suggests a blocker on a Must-have), a score of 2 (suggests major) or a red time (suggests minor) has a lower or missing severity.

Severity Meaning
4 — Blocker Stops the user completing a Must-have task. Fix before the next round.
3 — Major Serious difficulty or repeated errors. Task completes, but painfully.
2 — Minor Slows the user down or causes momentary confusion.
1 — Cosmetic Noticed and mentioned, but did not affect the outcome.

Scoring, part 2: Emotion

Logged per step, not per task. This is the important difference.

Emotion does not belong to a task — it belongs to a component. Inside a single task, the input field might delight someone and the confirmation button might frustrate them thirty seconds later. If you score the task as a whole, those cancel out and you learn nothing. If you log them separately, you know exactly what to leave alone and exactly what to fix.

So: whenever you see a reaction, log it at the step where it happened, in that step's Emotion and Type columns, and say in the Note which element caused it.

One emotion per participant per step: the strongest reaction you saw. If there were two, log the stronger one and describe both in the Note.

Score Reaction What you might observe
+2 Delight Surprised and pleased Laughs, says "oh, nice", does it again unprompted, calls someone over to look
+1 Positive Satisfied Smiles, nods, unprompted approving comment, visible confidence
0 Neutral No reaction It worked and they did not think about it. For a Must-have, this is success.
−1 Friction Mildly irritated Frowns, sighs, tries twice, mutters, goes quiet
−2 Frustration Actively annoyed Says so out loud, pushes it away, criticises it, stops trying

You cannot log a score without logging what you saw

Every emotional entry needs the observed behaviour that justifies it. "Seemed to like it" is not data — you cannot compare it across rounds and you cannot act on it. "Pressed it three more times without being asked, said 'that's satisfying'" is data.

Capture one verbatim quote per emotional event. Their words, not your summary of their words.

Separate interaction emotion from form emotion

Your prototype gets significantly more attractive between round 1 and round 3. A CMF'd model earns reactions that a glue-gunned one never will, and that has nothing to do with whether your design improved.

So tag every emotional entry as one or the other:

  • Interaction — reaction to what happened. "It responded instantly." "I pressed it twice and nothing." This is comparable across rounds.
  • Form — reaction to how it looks or feels. "That's beautiful." "The plastic feels cheap." Log it, learn from it, but it does not count as improvement, because the object itself changed.

Only interaction emotion goes into your round-over-round comparison. Confusing the two is one of the most common mistakes designers make about their own work.

A Must-have cannot score Delight

Nobody is delighted that the power button works. They expect it. Must-haves are noticed only when they fail — working, they score Neutral, and that is the correct result.

Delight comes from things the user did not expect. If every Must-have in your test is scoring +2, you are recording your own enthusiasm rather than theirs.

  3. A score, a time, a severity and a note for every step. Every score gets a note. When you saw a reaction, an Emotion and a Type too, with what you saw and the participant's words in the Note.

How each round differs

Round 1 — broad (Week 9)

Cover the whole hero experience shallowly: 3–5 tasks covering every Must-have in it, minimal depth. You are mapping where the pain is, not solving it. Expect this round to be uncomfortable — rough prototypes fail in obvious ways, and that is what makes them cheap to learn from. Include your naive participant here. Establish your baseline scores.

Round 2 — narrowing (Week 10, over the break)

Run a fresh dry run on the new prototype first (UT2 Dry Run): the object changed, so your own times change too. Re-test what scored well quickly, just to confirm it held. Spend the time you save going deeper where round 1 showed low scores or high severity: probe harder, watch more closely. Keep the round 1 steps exactly as they were and score them the same way. Where you want more detail, add finer sub-steps as extra columns inside their step's group: score them, but leave them out of the task mean, so round 2 still compares with round 1. Name a sub-step after its step, for example S2a and S2b under S2, so they are easy to leave out of the mean. The large blind spots should be gone; what remains is functionality that is still struggling.

Round 3 — targeted (Week 12)

Run a fresh dry run on the new prototype first (UT3 Dry Run), because the object changed again. Focus on what still scored badly. Same tasks, same rubric, so improvement is measurable rather than asserted. 6–8 participants, from outside product and industrial design. By now most problems have been combed out, and you are working on the hard ones. This is also the first round where desirability can be tested honestly, because it is the first time the object looks like a product.

What you submit

With your final project, the night before you present in Week 13:

  1. Your dry run and your UT table for all three rounds, as tabs in your HMI document
  2. The round-over-round comparison — task scores and emotion per step, in its "Comparison" tab
  3. An iteration log: what you found, what you changed, what happened to the score afterwards

This is worth 20 of the 100 points on your final project. The points are not for running three sessions — they are for showing that the rounds narrowed, that the scores are comparable, and that specific changes came from specific findings.

The strongest evidence you can present is a finding that surprised you. Something you were confident about that a participant broke, and what you did about it.

Before your first session

  • Tasks written out, taken from your HMI document, with priority marked
  • Dry run timed for every step, in your UT1 Dry Run tab
  • "UT1 Dry Run" and "UT1" set up as new tabs in your HMI document, ready to write into
  • A way to capture what happens — phone on a stand is fine. Ask first.
  • A second person if possible: one runs the session, one records
  • Your naive participant lined up for round 1

Two things not to do. Do not explain how it works before they start — whatever you explain is a thing you can no longer test. And do not defend it while they struggle — the silence is uncomfortable and it is where the finding lives.
