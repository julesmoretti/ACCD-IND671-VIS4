Introduction

This week your project becomes something a stranger can pick up and use. It will be rough: breadboard, cardboard, tape, glue gun. That is the point. A rough prototype is cheap to change, and the first round of testing will tell you what to change before you spend hours on a 3D print.

1. What a Low-Fidelity Prototype Has to Do

  • Every Must-have task works end to end: device input → WebSocket → screen, and screen → device. Faking is fine for anything that is not a Must-have.
  • A stranger can hold it. The breadboard and Arduino are fixed inside or onto a rough body (foam core, cardboard, a box) so nothing comes loose when someone handles it.
  • Inputs and outputs are roughly where they will end up. A button on the side of a box tests reach and grip; a button on a loose breadboard tests nothing.
  • Icons and labels are drawn on with a marker. Testing whether they are understood is part of round 1.

2. Making It Survive a Test

  • Tape or hot-glue the breadboard and Arduino down. Most round 1 failures are wires pulled out, not design problems.
  • Use stranded wire (tinned ends) for anything that reaches a part the user touches, so it can flex.
  • Label every wire at both ends with tape. When something stops working mid-test, you need to find it in seconds.
  • Power: a USB cable to your laptop is fine for round 1. Leave enough slack that the user can hold the device naturally.
  • Start the server, the React app and the Arduino in that order, and check the connection before each participant. Keep a note of the start-up steps so you do not forget one under pressure.

3. Round 1 Testing

The full instrument is on the User Testing Rubric & Protocol page. Read the sections Round 1 — broad and Before your first session. The scoring is the same as Week 4; what changes:

  • 4–5 participants, classmates, and one naive participant who has no idea what your device is.
  • Tasks come from your HMI document, each broken into steps, with priority marked and an expected time.
  • Broad, not deep: every Must-have task once, quickly. You are mapping where the problems are, not solving them.
  • Separate reactions to the interaction from reactions to the form. In round 1, people will react to the cardboard. Log it, but do not let it hide interaction problems.

4. Start Thinking in 3D Now

Next week the prototype moves into a 3D-printed form. Prints take time and the first one rarely fits, so start now:

  • Measure every part with calipers: Arduino, modules, buttons, displays, battery if any. Record length, width, height, and hole positions.
  • Block out the enclosure in CAD as simple shapes: where each part sits, where each input and output breaks the surface.
  • Leave it rough. Round 1 will tell you where things need to move.
