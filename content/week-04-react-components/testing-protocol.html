Software Testing Protocol

You are going to watch someone use the interface you have been building, and you are going to write down what happens. Not what you hoped would happen.

This is a short run at something you will do properly three times in the second half of the term. The scoring you learn here is the same scoring you will use in Weeks 9 to 12 — you are learning the instrument on something low-stakes before your grade depends on it.

What you are testing

Your project interface, as it stands. Not the Number Adder.

It does not need to be finished. It needs to be usable enough that someone can attempt a task without you narrating over their shoulder.

Where the tasks come from

The requirements list you wrote in Week 3. Each statement is a task:

As a user, when I enter a target number and press start, I expect the interface to show a live count and a stop button, so that I can monitor progress and interrupt it if something goes wrong.

becomes: "Set a target of 40 and start it running."

Pick 3–4 tasks. Prioritise your Must-haves. Write each one as an instruction with no method in it — "set a target of 40", not "type 40 in the target field and click start."

Participants

3–4 classmates. One at a time, not as a group.

This is alpha testing — in-house, with people who share your context. It is the easiest possible audience, which is the point: if a task fails here, it will fail everywhere.

Running a session

Fifteen minutes each is plenty.

  1. Tell them what the project is in one sentence. Do not explain the interface.
  2. Give them the first task. Then stop talking.
  3. Watch. Write down what they do, where they pause, what they try that does not work.
  4. If they get properly stuck, wait — count to ten — then offer the smallest possible hint. Record that you did.
  5. After each task, ask one question: "what did you expect to happen?"

Two things not to do

Do not explain how it works first. Anything you explain is something you can no longer test.

Do not defend it while they struggle. The urge is powerful and the silence is uncomfortable. Sit in it. That silence is where the finding is.

Scoring, part 1: Functionality

One row per participant per task.

Score Outcome What it looks like
4 Completed unaided Straight through, no pause, no wrong turns
3 Completed with hesitation Got there, but paused, backtracked, or tried something wrong first
2 Completed with help Needed a hint from you to proceed
1 Abandoned or failed Gave up, or believed they had succeeded when they had not

That last case is worth watching for. Someone who thinks they finished but did not is a worse result than someone who gives up — your interface told them something untrue.

Severity

Rate every issue you observe:

4 Blocker — stops a Must-have task completing · 3 Major — serious difficulty or repeated errors · 2 Minor — slows them down or causes momentary confusion · 1 Cosmetic — noticed and mentioned, no effect on outcome

Scoring, part 2: Emotion

Logged per moment, against the specific element that caused it — not per task.

Inside one task, the input field might please someone and the confirm button might irritate them twenty seconds later. Score the task as a whole and those cancel out. Log them separately and you know precisely what to leave alone and what to fix.

Score Reaction What you might observe
+2 Delight Surprised and pleased Laughs, "oh nice", does it again unprompted
+1 Positive Satisfied Smiles, nods, unprompted approving comment
0 Neutral No reaction It worked and they did not think about it
−1 Friction Mildly irritated Frowns, sighs, tries twice, goes quiet
−2 Frustration Actively annoyed Says so out loud, criticises it, stops trying

Neutral is not a bad score. Nobody is delighted that a form field accepts text — they expect it. Must-haves working correctly score 0, and that is the right answer. Delight comes from things people did not expect.

You cannot record a score without recording what you saw

"Seemed to like it" is not data. "Clicked it three more times without being asked, said 'that's satisfying'" is data. Every emotional entry needs an observed behaviour and one verbatim quote — their words, not your paraphrase.

Recording

Table A — Functionality

Task Priority P# Outcome Severity What happened
T1 Set a target of 40 Must P2 3 — hesitation 2 Typed 40, then looked for a submit button for several seconds before pressing Enter. Was not sure it had registered.

Table B — Emotion

Element P# Score Observed Verbatim
Live counter P2 +2 Leaned in when it started counting, watched it the whole way "Oh that's satisfying."
Target field P2 −1 Hunted for a button, hesitated, eventually pressed Enter uncertainly "Do I press something?"

Those two rows tell you what to do: leave the counter alone, give the target field a visible submit affordance. That is the whole point of the exercise.

What you bring to the midterm

For your Week 5 presentation:

  1. Your requirements list with priorities
  2. Tables A and B, filled in
  3. Three findings — what you saw, what it means, what you are going to do about it
  4. At least one thing you have already changed as a result

The most valuable thing you can bring is a finding that surprised you — something you were confident about that a participant broke. Those are worth more in a critique than a session where everything went smoothly, because a session where everything went smoothly usually means the tasks were too easy or you helped too much.

What changes in Week 9

Same scales, three additions:

  • Three rounds instead of one, compared against each other to show measurable improvement
  • Participants move outward — classmates, then other GradID terms, then students outside the discipline
  • Prototype fidelity rises across the rounds, which means separating reactions to the interaction from reactions to the form

None of that is new scoring. It is the same instrument, run properly.
