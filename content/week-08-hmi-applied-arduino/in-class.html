Today you apply HMI documentation to your own project: how your product talks to its user, on the device and on screen, written down in your HMI spreadsheet. Bring your Week 3 requirements list, your Week 4 test findings, your Week 7 hardware overview, and your Number Adder.

Review (20 min)

  • Hardware overview: your table and block diagram. Flag any pin count over the UNO R4's 14 digital and 6 analog pins.

Watch Me: What an HMI Document Looks Like (30 min)

  1. An industry example: part of the HMI sheet for an Astro gaming headset. How rows and categories are organised, and how button presses, LED patterns and voice prompts are written down.
  2. A past student example: LUMI, a projector and floor lamp in one product. What works: clear categories, every row written as a user need, a motor flagged as Complex. Exercise 1: Your Hero Experience as Rows (15 min)

Walk through your hero experience from start to finish. Every time the user does something, write it as a row with the first four columns of the UX tab. These rows go straight into your spreadsheet in Exercise 4.

Interaction User Needs Interaction Type Feedback (device / screen)
Set target As a user, when I turn the dial, I expect the target to change, so that I can choose how many turns to count. Physical dial Device: ring LEDs fill to match. Screen: "Target: 40".

Check each row for both halves: if the device responds but the screen does not, or the other way round, decide whether that is on purpose.

Break (10 min)

Exercise 2: States and Feedback (25 min)

  1. List every state your device can be in. Most products have at least: off, idle/ready, working, done, and error. Add disconnected: what happens when the WebSocket drops?
  2. For each state, decide how the user knows they are in it, on the device and on screen. Use more than one channel where it matters:
    • Visual: LED colour, blink pattern, display, screen
    • Auditory: tone, click, silence
    • Haptic: vibration, detent, resistance
  3. Check: can two states be confused? If "working" and "error" both blink, the user cannot tell them apart.
  4. Check your hardware overview: does every piece of feedback have a part that produces it? If not, add the part to your overview and your order list now.

Exercise 3: Run-Through in Pairs (20 min)

One person plays the device; the other is the user. The user tries the hero experience using your interface on screen. The "device" responds out loud and by hand: "the ring lights up to 40", "it beeps twice". No explaining.

Swap. Write down every moment where the user did not know what had happened. Those are missing or unclear feedback.

Exercise 4: Build Your HMI Spreadsheet (40 min)

You already have most of what goes in it. This exercise puts it in one place, in the format of the reading (section 3).

  1. Set up your Google Sheet with the two tabs: Product Information (including the hardware and software diagrams) and UX, with the UX columns from the reading.
  2. Paste in your hero experience rows from Exercise 1, under their own category.
  3. Add one row for each requirement on your Week 3 requirements list. Keep its priority: Must have, Nice to have, or Dropped (with the reason in Notes). Move any that now feel in between to Should have.
  4. Add one row for each interaction in your Week 7 hardware overview that is not on the list yet. The last column of the overview tells you what each row must say.
  5. Add the states and feedback from Exercise 2, and fix every gap Exercise 3 found.
  6. Group the rows into categories (for example Power, Setup, Main task, Errors), and give each row a Prototype Complexity.
  7. Finish at least the category that holds your hero experience. The rest is homework.

Your Week 4 test findings belong in the Notes column of the rows they affect.

Exercise 5: Plan the End-to-End Breadboard (10 min)

Look at your hero experience rows. For each one, list the part and the WebSocket message you need to make it happen on the breadboard, device to screen and screen to device. That list is your homework.
