The Project

Take a project you have already designed in a studio course — one that was resolved as an object, but never as an interaction. Most studio work stops at form, material, and intent. It assumes the thing works. It rarely asks how a person would actually operate it, what the device would tell them back, or whether any of it holds up when a real user puts their hands on it.

This term you make it work, and then you find out whether it works for anyone else.

From Week 6 onward you will build your chosen project into a functioning hardware-software prototype: Arduino on the device side, a React interface on the control side, and bi-directional WebSocket communication between them. You will document the Human-Machine Interface, run successive rounds of user testing against a shared rubric, and act on what the testing tells you.

What You Will Deliver

1. A working end-to-end prototype

Hardware and software communicating in both directions. The interface reflects the state of the device; the device responds to the interface. It must run live in Week 13 — a video of it working is not a substitute.

Define a hero experience: the single interaction that matters most in your project. That is what gets built well, tested hard, and demonstrated. Breadth is worth less than one interaction that genuinely works.

2. HMI documentation

A document covering both halves:

  • Product information — what the device is, who it is for, its intended use, and a labelled diagram
  • User experience — the interactions available, the user needs each one serves, the interaction types, the feedback the device gives back (visual, auditory, haptic), priority, and how software integrates

Version 1 is due in Week 8. The final version, updated with everything the final presentation feedback changed, is due the week after you present.

3. Three rounds of user testing

Task-based, scored against the course rubric, with the same rubric applied each time so scores are comparable.

  • Round 1 (Week 9) — broad. Cover the whole experience shallowly. Find where the pain is.
  • Round 2 (Week 10, over the break) — narrowing. Re-test what scored well quickly; spend the time you save going deeper where confidence was low.
  • Round 3 (Week 12) — targeted. Focus on what scored badly in rounds 1 and 2. Same tasks, same rubric, so improvement is measurable rather than asserted.

Submit the rubric scores across all three rounds, and an iteration log connecting specific findings to specific changes you made.

4. Final presentation

A live demonstration in Week 13, on Friday, December 11. Cover:

  • Who this is for — specifically, with evidence, not "general consumers"
  • What the project was before, and what it is now
  • A working demonstration of the hero experience
  • What the testing told you — including the findings you did not expect
  • What changed as a result, and what you would change next

How This Is Graded

The final project is 60% of your course grade, scored out of 100 on four criteria. The right-hand column is what full marks looks like:

Criterion Points Full marks looks like
Working prototype and technical integration 35 The hero experience runs live, from start to finish, with no help and no restart. Device and interface talk in both directions: the interface reflects the state of the device, and the device responds to the interface. The Arduino sketch and React app are in your repo.
HMI design and documentation 30 Both tabs complete. Every input and output has a row, feedback is specific enough to build from, and errors and disconnection are covered. The document shows your reasoning, and the final version reflects what testing and presentation feedback changed.
User testing and iteration evidence 20 Three rounds, same tasks, same rubric, so the scores can be compared. The iteration log links specific findings to specific changes, and the areas that started weak show measurable improvement.
Presentation quality 15 A specific target user, with evidence. Clear before and after. A working demo. Honest findings, including the ones you did not expect, and what you would change next.

Read this before you start building, not the week before you present. Full marks are not reachable by polishing at the end.

Completeness drives the grade. A prototype that runs unassisted through its hero experience, backed by three comparable rounds of testing and documentation that shows the reasoning, will score well. A prototype that needs a restart mid-demo, or testing that was run once and described anecdotally, will not — regardless of how resolved the object looks.
